# EU Homologation: Making a Chinese EV Road-Legal

Taxation gets an EV *into* the EU. Homologation is what makes it legal to *drive*. These are separate problems, and conflating them is one of the most common — and most costly — mistakes a first-time importer makes.

> **Scope note.** This page describes the approval framework in general terms for planning purposes. Approval routes, thresholds, and national procedures change; the exact route for your vehicle is determined by the destination member state's approval authority. Treat this as a map, not a ruling — confirm the current state before ordering.

## The three approval routes

The EU offers three broad paths to make an imported vehicle road-legal, distinguished mainly by volume.

### 1. EU Whole Vehicle Type Approval (WVTA)

The standard route for manufacturers importing at scale. A WVTA certifies that a vehicle type meets all applicable EU technical requirements across the single market, on the basis of the EU type-approval framework. It is expensive and process-heavy, and generally only makes sense for a manufacturer or a large-volume importer.

### 2. Small Series Type Approval

For importers bringing in a limited annual volume (the exact threshold varies by vehicle category), a Small Series approval relaxes some requirements. It is the practical middle ground for specialist importers — the paperwork burden sits between full WVTA and the single-vehicle route, and it stays valid across the single market within its quota.

### 3. National single-vehicle approval

For individual vehicles, each EU member state offers a route to approve a single car against adapted requirements:

- **Germany:** §21 StVZO approval via a technical inspection organisation (TÜV, DEKRA, GTÜ).
- **Netherlands, France, and others:** each has an analogous single-vehicle procedure.

This is the most common route for private imports and low-volume dealer imports, because it is far cheaper than full type approval. Its limitation: a national single-vehicle approval is generally valid in the approving member state, and recognition in another member state is not automatic — if you plan to move or resell the vehicle across borders, confirm portability up front.

## What a China-market EV typically fails

A vehicle built for the Chinese domestic market is not automatically compliant in the EU. Common gaps include:

- **Lighting conformity** — headlamp beam pattern and light signatures may not meet EU requirements.
- **Speedometer and display** — must show km/h in the correct format; Chinese-market displays may differ.
- **Electrical and safety standards** — charging port interlock, safety systems, and homologated component conformity.
- **Noise and environmental** — the EU requires compliance with relevant acoustic and environmental standards.

Each gap is either a documentation exercise or a physical modification, and the physical ones are where cost and time accumulate.

## Documentation that shortens the route

The cheapest approval is the one where the vehicle already carries the evidence. Before ordering, ask the supplier for:

1. **EU Certificate of Conformity (CoC)** — the single most valuable document if it exists for your VIN. Verify the VIN range, not just the model name.
2. **Existing type-approval certificate** — some Chinese brands approve export-market variants themselves; the approval number lets a technical service check coverage directly.
3. **Component conformity documents** — homologated lighting, charging port, and safety-component evidence shortens the adaptation list.

If none of these exist, budget the full single-vehicle route and get a written conformity assessment from an accredited technical service **before** paying for the vehicle. A car that cannot be economically approved is worth zero to you — discovering that after payment is the expensive way to learn it.

## Cost and timeline

For a small-volume or single-vehicle import, budget:

- **Cost:** $1,500–$5,000, depending on the vehicle and the extent of adaptation.
- **Timeline:** 2–6 weeks, longer if parts need to be sourced.

These are planning figures, not quotes: a vehicle needing rare lighting parts or repeated inspection attempts can exceed both. Don't shortcut this step — a vehicle that fails its first approval attempt costs more in re-testing and rework than doing it correctly the first time.
